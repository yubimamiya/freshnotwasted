Group 3 · Fresh, Not Wasted

A forecasting system that tells each store how much fresh food to stock.

Stores lose money two ways on fresh food: ordering too much (it spoils) and ordering too little (empty shelves). FreshCast predicts demand for every item in every store, turns that into an order, and tells staff what to mark down or donate before it goes to waste. This page walks through how it works, one step at a time, using the running prototype.

Results from a test on 4 weeks the model never saw, compared with a simple "order what sold the same weekday last week + 10%" rule. Simulated data: 3 stores, 24 fresh items, about 3 years of daily sales. Use the store and scenario controls at the top. Every live section updates.

Step 1

1Collect the data

The model is only as good as what it sees. A real store already produces most of this. Our prototype simulates each source so we could build and test the full system.

Sales

Point-of-sale data per item, per store, per day.

Key fix: when a shelf goes empty at 3 PM, sales understate real demand. Shelf cameras record when it ran out, so we can estimate what would have sold.

Stock by expiry date

Date-coded barcodes or RFID tags show not just "40 units" but "12 expire tomorrow".

Needed for markdowns and donations.

Calendar and promotions

Holidays, paydays, planned price cuts.

Known in advance, so they're the most reliable signals.

Weather and storm alerts

Temperature forecasts and National Weather Service warnings.

Storms cause a buying rush before and a drop during.

Trucks

Delivery schedule and delay status from truck GPS.

Tells the model how many days the stock has to last.

Social media trends

How often a food topic is being posted about, tracked over time.

Example: "Dubai chocolate" posts → more strawberry sales a few days later.

Step 3

3Forecast demand, one model per product

Each product gets its own model, because a turkey and a banana react very differently to the same day. The model gives a best guess and a range: the margin of error.

How the model works

  • Model: regression on the log of sales, so effects multiply (e.g. "Saturday ×1.25" and "Thanksgiving week ×4").
  • Trained on all history up to last night, about 1,000 days per product per store.
  • Range: taken from how far off the model was on past days (10th to 90th percentile).
  • Production version: same features with gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM).

Step 4

4Decide how much to stock

The forecast says what's likely. The order also depends on which mistake costs more for that item: running out or throwing food away.

The rule

target in-stock level = cost of running out ÷ (cost of running out + cost of waste)
stock target = expected sales + z(target) × uncertainty
order = stock target − usable stock on hand → round to cases
  • Cost of running out = lost profit, plus a penalty for staples like milk and bread.
  • Cost of waste = what we paid, minus what markdowns and donations recover.
  • Usable stock = units that won't expire before they can sell.
  • Normal day: cover until tomorrow's truck. Storm: cover until trucks return.

Step 5

5Deal with surplus before it spoils

For every batch expiring today or tomorrow, the system compares units on the shelf with the units expected to sell first (oldest stock sells first).

1. Predict leftovers

Leftover = batch size − (expected sales before expiry − older stock ahead of it).

2. Mark down

If at least 15% of the batch is likely left over: 30% off, or 50% if more than half is likely left over.

3. Donate the rest

The nearest partner that can take that food type (meat needs cold storage) is notified today for a 9 AM pickup.

The twist

Winter storm tomorrow, trucks delayed 48 hours

The same seven steps run. Only the inputs change, and the system adapts on its own.

Forecast

Storm features fire: a buying rush on bread, milk, eggs and meat today, a drop during the storm.

Order window

Stock must now last 3 days instead of 1. For essentials short of that, it asks for a same-day top-up and sets a 2-per-customer limit.

Surplus

Markdowns stop on essentials. Items whose sales drop in the storm, like sushi, go to markdown or donation sooner.

Staff

Adds tasks: release backroom stock in 3 rounds, bake extra bread in store, put up limit signs.

Testing

Does it work? A fair test against the current rule

We trained the models only on data from before each test period, then replayed those days. Both approaches saw the same customers, same shelf lives and same truck delays.

Limits and next steps

What this prototype shows, and what it doesn't

Limits

  • All data is simulated. Results show the approach works, not exact savings in a real store.
  • The "current rule" is a simple stand-in, not Walmart's actual ordering system.
  • Shelf lives, task minutes and donation rates are assumptions.

Next steps

  • Pilot in a few stores with real sales and shelf-camera data.
  • Switch to gradient-boosted trees and update forecasts during the day as sales come in.
  • Move stock between nearby stores when one has extra.
  • Connect donation notices to food banks' scheduling systems.
